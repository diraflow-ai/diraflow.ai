What makes a great preference annotator? Insights from 1,000 calibration tasks

Dembe J.
Lead Research Engineer
Mar 10, 2026 · 9 min read

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is the secret sauce behind many of today’s most capable LLMs. But the quality of the final model depends almost entirely on the quality of the preference data — and that means the people providing the feedback. Over the past year, Diraflow collected and analysed calibration data from over 1,000 preference annotators across dozens of projects. We measured everything: consistency, response time, preference direction, sensitivity to subtle differences, and drift over time. Here’s what we learned about what separates a great preference annotator from an average one.

Calibration first: not all preferences are equal

Before any annotator touches production data, we run a calibration suite of 50–100 gold‑standard preference pairs. These pairs are carefully curated to include clear winners, ties, and subtle trade‑offs (e.g., more helpful but less safe). By analysing performance on this calibration set, we can predict overall annotator quality with surprising accuracy. The single best predictor? Consistency on near‑tie examples. Annotators who can repeatedly pick the same side when the difference is small are also more reliable on ambiguous real‑world data.

# Example calibration metric: preference stability
For each annotator, present the same subtle preference pair twice, 3 days apart.
Stability = % of pairs where the same side is chosen.
Top annotators: >85% stability on subtle pairs.
Average annotators: ≈55% (close to random).

The paradox of speed: faster is not worse

Conventional wisdom says slower annotators are more careful. Our data shows a different story. The fastest quartile of annotators (median response time < 8 seconds per preference) had higher calibration accuracy than the slowest quartile (> 25 seconds). Why? Fast annotators often have more experience and rely on intuition honed by clear guidelines, while slow annotators might overthink or get distracted. However, extremely fast (< 3 seconds) correlates with random clicking. The sweet spot: 6–12 seconds per pair, with low variance.

Drift and fatigue: the hidden destroyers

Annotator quality is not static. In long sessions (over 90 minutes), we observed a steady decline in preference consistency: the probability of reversing a previously seen pair increased by 18% after the 80th minute. Even worse, some annotators develop “position bias” — preferring the left response more often over time. Great annotators are aware of their own drift; they take breaks, request fresh calibration checks, and use keyboard shortcuts to avoid mouse‑induced side bias. Our most successful teams enforce mandatory 10‑minute breaks every 60 minutes and run a 10‑item recalibration after each break.

“We used to think that hiring domain experts was enough. But after analysing our annotator data, we realised that consistency on subtle pairs and self‑awareness of bias were more predictive of final model win rate than any credential.” — Head of Data, frontier LLM lab

What about domain expertise?

For general preference (e.g., “which response is more helpful?”), generic annotators with strong calibration scores performed as well as domain experts. But for specialised domains like medical advice or legal reasoning, subject‑matter expertise became critical — but only when paired with calibration. A brilliant doctor who is inconsistent on preference ties is worse than a moderately knowledgeable annotator with perfect stability. We now run a two‑stage filter: first calibrate on generic preference pairs, then test on a small set of domain‑specific gold standards.

Practical takeaways for building a preference annotation team

  • Calibrate, calibrate, calibrate. Use a held‑out set of subtle pairs and reject annotators with stability < 70% on repeats.
  • Monitor response time distributions. Flag anyone who consistently finishes in < 4 seconds or > 30 seconds for review.
  • Bake in forced breaks and position‑randomisation. Swap left/right display order randomly to counter side bias.
  • Use “attention checks” — obvious preference pairs (e.g., a helpful response vs. gibberish). Low performance on these is a red flag.
  • Reward consistency, not speed. Bonus structures that encourage high stability often improve overall data quality more than volume bonuses.

Case study: from average to elite in 4 weeks

One of our clients had an annotator pool with mediocre calibration (average stability 62% on subtle pairs). We introduced weekly 30‑minute calibration sessions where annotators discussed disagreements on the same subtle pairs, plus a dashboard showing each person’s consistency trends. Within four weeks, average stability rose to 81%, and the downstream RLHF model showed a 9% improvement in win rate against a baseline. The key was turning calibration from a one‑time test into a continuous feedback loop.

The future: AI‑assisted preference annotation

We are now experimenting with models that can pre‑label easy preference pairs and surface only the ambiguous ones for human annotators. This hybrid approach can increase annotator focus and reduce fatigue. But even with AI assistance, the core human traits we identified — consistency, bias awareness, and calibration stability — remain essential. A great preference annotator is not just a labeler; they are a key component of the RLHF system, and investing in their skills pays off directly in model performance.

If you’re building an RLHF pipeline, don’t treat annotators as interchangeable workers. Measure them, calibrate them, and learn from the best. The data will thank you — and so will your model.
