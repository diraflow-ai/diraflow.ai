The hidden complexity of mathematical reasoning datasets

Rafael Santos
Lead Data Scientist, STEM
Mar 28, 2026 · 5 min read

At first glance, building datasets for mathematical reasoning seems straightforward: collect math problems and their solutions. But as soon as you try to train a model that can truly reason – not just pattern-match – you hit a wall of hidden complexity. Over the last two years, Diraflow has constructed over 200,000 annotated math examples spanning algebra, number theory, calculus, and proof-based contest problems. Here’s what we learned about the messy reality behind “math data”.

Notation is not neutral

A single mathematical concept can be expressed in dozens of notationally distinct ways. \( \frac{dy}{dx} \) vs \( f'(x) \) vs \( \dot{y} \). \( \binom{n}{k} \) vs \( C(n,k) \) vs \( {}_nC_k \). Models trained on one notation family often fail to generalise to others, even though the underlying mathematics is identical. In one experiment, we found that a model fine-tuned on Leibniz notation for derivatives scored 89% accuracy on test questions using the same notation, but only 61% on identical problems written with prime notation. That’s not reasoning — that’s overfitting to typography.

Our solution: we built a systematic notation augmentation pipeline. Each seed problem is rewritten in 3–5 equivalent notational variants by expert mathematicians, then verified for semantic equivalence. This increased dataset size and dramatically improved cross-notation generalisation during fine-tuning.

Proof granularity: the missing layer

Most math datasets are question–answer pairs: the model outputs a final numeric answer or a short expression. But real mathematical reasoning is about chains of justification. A model that outputs “42” might have guessed, or might have derived it correctly. To train genuine reasoning, we need step-level annotations. We developed a proof-step taxonomy that labels each inference line as “definition application”, “algebraic manipulation”, “inductive hypothesis”, “contradiction” etc. Using this, we built datasets where the target is not just the answer but a structured proof sketch. Models trained on this data show much stronger performance on out-of-distribution proof problems and can explain their reasoning when asked.

# Example of a step-annotated math problem (simplified JSON)

  "problem": "Prove that the sum of two even integers is even.",

    {"text": "Let a = 2k, b = 2m for some integers k,m", "type": "definition"},

}

Error taxonomies: why wrong answers matter

One of the most valuable parts of a math dataset is the negative examples – but not all errors are equal. A student (or model) might make a slip in arithmetic, confuse a sign, misapply a theorem, or commit a logical fallacy. Each of these error types requires a different pedagogical or training intervention. We built an error taxonomy with 18 categories and had annotators label not only whether a model’s output was correct, but why it was wrong. This turned out to be critical for RLHF: we could give partial credit for correct reasoning despite a numeric mistake, and strongly penalise logical leaps.

“Using Diraflow’s error-annotated dataset, we reduced the rate of algebraic sign errors in our model by 42% in three weeks of fine-tuning, while keeping final-answer accuracy high.” – Head of Research, AI math tutoring startup

Diversity of solution strategies

A common mistake in math dataset collection is to assume there is one “canonical” solution per problem. In reality, many problems can be solved in multiple ways – analytic vs. combinatorial, forward vs. backward, using different theorems. If your training data only contains one solution path, the model learns that path as a rigid template. When it encounters a novel problem that would benefit from an alternative approach, it gets stuck. We therefore intentionally collect multiple solution strategies for at least 30% of our problems, often by using different expert annotators and then reconciling. This multi-strategy data has been shown to improve model robustness and creativity.

Operational best practices for math datasets

  • Use LaTeX normalization. Before any comparison, canonicalize LaTeX: convert \( \frac{a}{b} \) to \frac{a}{b}, unify spacing, etc. This reduces false negatives during evaluation.
  • Include non-numeric answers. Proofs, derivations, and “no solution” responses are underrepresented. Build separate evaluation rubrics for these.
  • Beware of data leakage. Many widely-used math problem sets are already in common pre-training corpora. We verify originality using n-gram overlap with GPT-4’s training data (as far as we can) and create original variants when needed.
  • Annotator domain expertise is non-negotiable. We only hire annotators with at least a Bachelor’s in mathematics or a related field, and we calibrate them weekly with gold-standard problems.

Beyond benchmarks: reasoning as a process

Ultimately, math datasets should not just measure whether a model can spit out the right number. They should teach and evaluate the process of reasoning: forming hypotheses, checking edge cases, constructing counterexamples. At Diraflow, we are now working on interactive proof environments where models can request verification of sub-steps – a form of agentic evaluation applied to mathematics. The hidden complexity we uncovered is not a bug; it’s a feature of how rich mathematical thinking really is. Embracing that complexity is the only way to build AI that truly understands math.
